> Sometimes the most powerful stories don't come from the glam and the glitz and big stages but from the
> courtyards of rural India. Mothers of Courage is one such story. This 8-minute documentary follows
> first-generation-literate mothers in rural Bihar who are taking a stand against child marriage and
> ensuring their daughters stay in school for the longest time. Their extraordinary journey has now
> earned global recognition — becoming India's only shortlisted entry in the Sustainable Development
> Goals category at the Cannes Lions. Joining us to take this conversation forward are two women behind
> this remarkable movement: Urmila Didi, whose grassroots work has transformed thousands of lives, and
> Khushboo Awasthi, Co-founder and CEO of ShikshaLokam.
>
> [Khushboo, on hearing the shortlist news] It was a very joyful moment — all of us were proud to be the
> only Indian entry in that category. But honestly and surprisingly, my first thought wasn't so much
> about the Cannes. It was more about the mothers, the women in Bihar. I have sat through the chaupals
> where these women have shared how they really don't believe that their voices matter — all their life
> they have seen decisions, the choices, made by others for them and for the girls in the family. Over
> the course of the chaupals I have also seen how the conversations have started shifting — they have
> started asking questions, identifying the problems and taking ownership to solve them; they have
> started believing in their own agency, their own ability to challenge the norms. We made this film
> primarily to spread this awareness, this message, among other women and community members — that their
> voices really do matter, and only if they take the commitment and take action towards it, things can
> change.

> [Khushboo, on the shift & systemic barriers] A great deal has changed. There was a time when girls
> completing their education wasn't even considered a normal thing — it was extraordinary. Today, even a
> parent in the most disadvantaged circumstances feels that girls have an equal right to education. One of
> the myths people hold, especially in urban areas like Delhi, is that people in rural areas don't care
> about education — that they feel it's optional. But in the last decade I have not met even a single
> parent who has told me "मेरे बच्चे के लिए शिक्षा जरूरी नहीं है." Everybody feels their child should have a
> better future, better opportunities than they had. It's not about lack of aspiration — there are
> systemic barriers: schools far away with no means of transport; children at home while both parents go
> to work and someone must care for younger siblings; guests arriving and someone needed to cook; lack of
> awareness about what to do after class 10; children who have completed graduation and not got jobs. These
> are systemic barriers that need all the actors — government, society, community, civil society — to come
> together, break them and remove them, so our girls can access the education they deserve.
>
> [Khushboo, on Shikshagraha] One very important aspect of any movement building is narrative building.
> The reason we called it Shikshagraha — it's not a program, it's not a campaign, it is a movement. Like
> people came together for our freedom — we designed Satyagraha — can we actually come together for the
> fight against education inequity: can we design Shikshagraha. There are already people in the system
> doing great jobs — like we found Urmila Didi and her network, and other very powerful, beautiful local
> partners doing fantastic work across Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Telangana, Tamil Nadu. The idea is: how do
> we all come together and create a larger narrative that this problem has to be solved, and solved for
> ever — because only when there is a collective voice and collective action does the narrative become
> strong enough for even government to take notice and to join hands. The power of such videos, the power
> of media, is in amplifying such stories — such stories of hope. Stories matter because they show you
> that things have changed, things are changing, and people have hope, people have not given up — and so
> we should not give up. That's the whole message and approach around Shikshagraha.
>
