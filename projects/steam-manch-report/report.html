Executive Summary

STEAM Manch 2025–26 was implemented across Haryana government schools in partnership with SCERT Haryana, with ThinkTac and ShikshaLokam serving as programme partners under the broader सक्षम हरियाणा initiative. The programme was delivered through four structured Micro-Improvement Project (MIP) cycles between November 2025 and February 2026, and concluded with the STEAM Mela showcase event in March 2026.

Designed as an experiential, curriculum-aligned learning programme for Grades 6–8, STEAM Manch integrated activities across Science, Mathematics, English, and Social Studies. The programme leveraged the DIKSHA platform to support teachers in implementing classroom activities and documenting participation through reflections, evidence submissions, and structured project workflows.

Programme Context
and Design

Background

STEAM Manch was implemented as part of the broader सक्षम हरियाणा initiative led by SCERT Haryana, with the objective of strengthening classroom practices that support experiential learning and the development of 21st-century skills in government schools.

Designed for Grades 6–8, the programme integrated learning experiences across Science, Mathematics, English, and Social Studies, while aligning with the intent of NEP 2020 and emerging curricular directions reflected in NCF 2023.

Within the partnership structure, ThinkTac led programme and pedagogy design, including development of the learning model, activity architecture, and implementation resources. ShikshaLokam supported programme implementation systems, stakeholder coordination, DIKSHA workflows, and monitoring processes.

The MIP Approach

The programme was delivered through Micro-Improvement Projects (MIPs) — a structured implementation model designed to encourage teachers to adopt small, achievable classroom practices that can be sustained and strengthened over time.

Each MIP cycle was hosted as a project workflow on DIKSHA, through which participating teachers accessed programme resources, implemented classroom activities, and submitted evidence of execution through structured tasks and reflections.

The underlying programme assumption was that consistent classroom experimentation and incremental improvement can collectively contribute to stronger student engagement and increased teacher confidence in facilitating experiential learning.

Programme Structure

STEAM Manch 2025–26 was implemented between November 2025 and March 2026, comprising four monthly MIP cycles, followed by the concluding STEAM Mela showcase. Each monthly cycle included:

  • One learning module for each grade (Grades 6, 7, and 8)
  • Four subject-linked activities per grade (Science, Mathematics, English, and Social Studies)
  • A total of 12 classroom activities per cycle
  • Teacher orientation sessions, facilitator support resources, and continuous communication support

Activities were intentionally designed to be curriculum-linked and low-resource, enabling implementation using locally available and everyday materials and reducing barriers to classroom participation.

Participation Trends Across Cycles

Project Submissions by Cycle

Submissions were highest during Cycle 1 (49,538), followed by relatively stable levels in Cycles 2 and 3 before a sharper decline in Cycle 4. Cycle 1 alone accounted for approximately 38% of total submissions.

This participation pattern should be interpreted within the context of programme implementation dynamics rather than as a simple indicator of reduced interest or effectiveness.

  • Cycle 1 (November 2025) benefited from initial programme mobilisation, teacher orientations, and broad onboarding efforts led through implementation channels, resulting in the programme's highest participation levels.
  • Cycles 2 and 3 (December 2025 – January 2026) recorded comparatively stable participation of approximately 32,000–34,000 submissions, suggesting continued engagement among a substantial teacher cohort after the launch phase.
  • Cycle 4 (February 2026) recorded 15,302 submissions — the most visible decline. This timing coincided with heightened academic and administrative demands within schools, including examination-related responsibilities.

Participation tapering across cycles is consistent with implementation patterns commonly observed in large-scale, voluntary or semi-voluntary professional engagement programmes, where sustained participation typically concentrates among a smaller group of consistently engaged participants over time.

STEAM Mela —
Programme Culmination

Overview

The STEAM Mela, conducted in March 2026, served as the culminating event of STEAM Manch 2025–26 and marked the closing phase of the programme cycle.

Conceptualised as a school-level celebration and documentation of learning, the STEAM Mela provided participating schools with an opportunity to showcase classroom experiences emerging from the four MIP cycles and make student learning visible to the wider school and community ecosystem.

The event was designed to extend programme participation beyond project submission by creating space for presentation, reflection, community engagement, and recognition of classroom efforts. Implementation was structured through a dedicated workflow on DIKSHA, where participating teachers documented planning, execution, and evidence of school-level activities through a series of defined tasks.

Participation

The STEAM Mela recorded participation from 1,284 unique teachers across 772 schools in 22 districts, making it one of the programme's most visible school-level engagement moments.

Participation records and teacher reflections submitted through DIKSHA suggest that schools used the Mela as an opportunity to extend classroom activities into broader school participation. An important observation from participation patterns is that STEAM Mela representation extended beyond only the highest-frequency MIP participants. Rather than functioning as a standalone event, the STEAM Mela acted as a culminating layer of the STEAM Manch implementation model, creating an opportunity to connect classroom activities with student expression, school participation, and community visibility — and recognising teacher and student participation in a collective setting.

Evaluation Framework

To recognise sustained and high-quality participation, a structured four-stage evaluation process was applied to identify top-performing teachers across STEAM Manch 2025–26. The framework was designed to balance scale with review quality, progressively narrowing the participant pool through a combination of participation data and qualitative assessment of implementation evidence.

It is important to note that this process was intended to identify programme exemplars and should not be interpreted as a measure of overall teacher effectiveness.

Challenges, Observations,
and Learnings

Participation Continuity Across Cycles

Submissions declined across successive cycles, most visibly in Cycle 4. Contributing factors include the initial mobilisation effect (early cycles benefit from broad onboarding), academic calendar pressures (examinations, assessments, administrative responsibilities), and platform and documentation demands of recurring digital submission workflows.

Geographic Distribution of Participation

Although the programme reached 23 districts, participation varied across geographies. Nuh, Fatehabad, and Sirsa collectively contributed a substantial share of total submissions, while several districts recorded comparatively lower volumes — suggesting an opportunity for more context-responsive district engagement strategies.

Variation in Evidence Submission Quality

Review of STEAM Mela documentation indicated variation in completeness and depth of submitted evidence across schools. This may reflect practical implementation realities rather than differences in commitment — reinforcing the value of workflows that balance quality expectations with practical ease of implementation.

Programme Strengths

  • Large-scale participation: 3,656 teachers across 1,547 schools demonstrates the feasibility of structured experiential learning at scale.
  • Curriculum-linked, low-resource design: Activities aligned with classroom realities and reduced barriers to participation.
  • Digital implementation records: DIKSHA supported structured documentation and programme monitoring at scale.
  • School and community visibility through STEAM Mela: The culminating event extended classroom experiences into broader school and community engagement.

Programme Spotlight

Nuh District: A High-Performing Example

Among participating districts, Nuh recorded the highest overall programme participation, with 27,963 activities uploaded across 260 schools. The district's participation profile is notable because engagement was spread across a wide network of schools rather than concentrated in a few places.

  • GSSS Chahalka (Nuh) recorded the highest number of total uploads in the district, with 812 total activities covering 63 different topics.
  • Ravinder (GMS Dingerheri, Taoru block) completed 20 different project topics in Maths and Science, and was recognised as a top teacher.

Mahendergarh and Fatehabad: Deep Classroom Engagement

While Nuh achieved the highest total uploads through its large school network, individual schools in Mahendergarh and Fatehabad led the state in curriculum completion depth, covering the widest variety of topics.

  • GMS Sisoth (Mahendergarh) came first in the entire state for topic coverage — 94 different project topics across 644 total uploads.
  • GHS Alipur Barota (Fatehabad) came second in the state, completing 91 different project topics across 590 total uploads.
  • GGSSS Duloth Ahir (Mahendergarh) combined scale and depth — 671 total uploads across 91 unique topics.
  • Saraswati Devi (GSSS Dhabi Kalan, Fatehabad) was recognised as an exemplar teacher for completing 11 full project topics across English and Social Science.

These examples highlight how individual institutions maximised the programme's framework to drive deep classroom engagement across different school contexts.

Recommendations
and Way Forward

Strengthening Programme Continuity

  • Align programme timelines with the academic calendar — avoid periods with concentrated examination and administrative responsibilities, enabling greater continuity across all cycles.
  • Introduce structured participation checkpoints — a mid-cycle review point (for example, after Cycle 2) to identify participation drop-offs early and support targeted re-engagement.
  • Reduce documentation friction where feasible — simplify evidence submission workflows while maintaining programme visibility.

Recognition and Sustained Motivation

  • Create structured recognition pathways — programme certificates, acknowledgement mechanisms, or district-level visibility for teachers demonstrating sustained participation.
  • Enable peer learning and teacher leadership — engage recognised teachers as peer contributors, mentors, or facilitators in future iterations.

Improving Reach and Equity Across Districts

  • Adopt differentiated district engagement approaches — context-responsive mobilisation and support strategies rather than uniform implementation across districts.
  • Build participation pathways beyond entry-level engagement — a phased model supporting progression from initial participation toward deeper, sustained engagement.

Strengthening Evidence and Programme Learning

  • Expand understanding beyond participation metrics — incorporate indicators that capture implementation quality and classroom experience alongside participation volume.
  • Generate deeper qualitative insights — focused conversations, reflections, or case documentation with selected teachers to inform future programme improvement.

Overall, future programme design may build on the strengths of sustained teacher participation while creating conditions that support wider continuity, stronger implementation experiences, and deeper evidence of engagement.

Conclusion

STEAM Manch 2025–26 demonstrates the potential of combining structured implementation cycles, experiential classroom practices, and digital programme workflows within Haryana's government school ecosystem.

Across the programme period, participation was recorded from 3,655 teachers, representing 1,527 schools across 23 districts, with more than 132,000 project submissions documented through DIKSHA. Together, these participation records provide a substantial programme dataset and reflect engagement with classroom activities across Science, Mathematics, English, and Social Studies for Grades 6–8.

The concluding STEAM Mela, which engaged 1,284 teachers across 772 schools, extended programme participation into a broader school-level showcase and created opportunities for schools to present classroom experiences and celebrate student engagement.

The programme also created pathways to recognise sustained participation, with 11 teachers identified through a structured evaluation process and 598 teachers continuing participation across all four MIP cycles. These participation patterns suggest opportunities for future investment in teacher recognition, peer learning, and continued implementation support.

Most importantly, STEAM Manch 2025–26 established and tested an operational model that combined monthly MIP cycles, curriculum-linked activities, and digital documentation mechanisms. The experience from this implementation provides useful inputs for strengthening future programme design and building greater continuity of participation over time.

As the programme evolves, the learnings from 2025–26 offer a foundation for strengthening teacher engagement, classroom participation, and evidence-informed programme improvement in the years ahead.
