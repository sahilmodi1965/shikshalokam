# Orchestrating Education System Change — the Oxford playbook on Shikshagraha

**Independent, peer-credible, publicly published research about Shikshagraha, by the University of
Oxford.** This is the strongest third-party evidence asset ShikshaLokam has. Use it whenever a
funder, government partner or awards jury needs external validation rather than our own claims.

- **Author:** Harry Bregazzi · **Published:** 29 June 2026 · Government Outcomes Lab (GO Lab),
  Blavatnik School of Government, University of Oxford
- **Supervisor / Principal Investigator:** Dr Mara Airoldi · research assistance: Chhavi Khandelwal
- **Academic advisory committee:** Bindu Thirumalai, Santhosh Mathew, Sujatha Rao, Rucha Pande
- **SL lead named in the announcement:** Neeraj Doddamane (titled Chief Strategy Officer there;
  now COO — see [[../concepts/leadership]])
- **Evidence base:** 30 interviews, plus focus groups and field observations in India, across
  **Bihar, Nagaland and Telangana**. Chaupals observed in Bihar and Karnataka.
- **Photos throughout the report are credited to ShikshaLokam Foundation.**

## The framing

Shikshagraha is presented as an example of what Alnoor Ebrahim calls an **ecosystem strategy** —
coordinated action by many actors towards a shared goal. The argument: the learning crisis is too
complex and too local for any single actor, including government, so solutions must involve multiple
actors and must vary by context. *"What is needed in Bihar is not the same as what is needed in
Nagaland."* Hence decentralisation plus enough coordination to avoid duplication.

## Three key considerations (the report's core findings)

**1. What's my role?** Partnerships work by combining complementary capacities.
- **Government** is best placed to: (1) cultivate a guiding vision and mindset for reform;
  (2) select partners; (3) implement policy at scale. All three flow from being the legitimate
  authority.
- **Civil society** provides: (1) connection to local context; (2) testing of new ideas and
  practice; (3) support where government lacks capacity.
- A state official's framing: government must work **"horizontally"** across a whole population,
  while CSOs work **"vertically"**, responding to the specificities of particular places.
- **Guiding vision was found to be a "missing ingredient"** in some partnerships — and it fades if
  it rests on one or two individuals rather than becoming collectively held.

**2. Working with the system.** Take a whole-system view, from state government down to individual
schools. A CSO's job is not to get government to accept its idea; the useful focus may be textbook
delivery, washroom cleanliness or budget utilisation instead. One CSO leader on the discipline
required: they have sometimes *"let go of what we wanted to do."*

**3. Routes towards trust.** Trust cannot be assumed; the baseline may be mistrust. Government can
read "civil society" as unaccountable activism; CSOs fear co-optation. Routes that worked:
- **Credibility and values.** One funder: they could accept a CSO missing targets, but *"there is
  no compromise"* on poor ethics. A compelling personal narrative from the CSO leader helps.
  With politicians: show no motive other than quality education, and avoid political association.
- **Demonstrating impact.** A CSO ran school showcases for government partners. Their first-year
  learning outcomes were *"not great"*, but officials could see change happening before it reached
  exam results.

## Three risks the report names (worth knowing we are on record about)

1. **Overdependence** of government on external organisations, weakening government's own capability.
2. **Burdening schools.** Uncoordinated partnerships stack up; teachers get trained on several
   activities at once, sometimes two or three CSOs are signed up to do the same thing in the same
   schools. Schools have complained they cannot finish the core curriculum. **One Indian state has
   recently stopped allowing CSOs to work directly in schools** because of disruption.
3. **Falling short of collective action**, through competition between CSOs (funding, access to
   government, credit) and through rigidity — pursuing one idea at the expense of responsiveness.
   Notably, CSO rigidity is sometimes traced to **donor** agendas.

**Antidotes suggested:** government should be intentional and selective about partners, integrate
CSOs within its own processes rather than in parallel, plan exit strategy from the outset, and set a
high-level measurable outcome no single organisation could achieve alone — which recalibrates
competition into collaboration.

## Shikshagraha facts the report puts on the public record

- Movement **active across fifteen Indian states** at time of writing.
- **Over 30,000 Shiksha Chaupals** organised. Problems raised in observed meetings ranged from poor
  school sanitation to **caste discrimination**.
- Chaupal insights are aggregated into a qualitative database of **"voices from the ground"** —
  public dashboard: https://dashboard.shikshagraha.org/voices-from-the-ground
- India has **more than one million government schools** (UDISE+, 2025).
- Donors interviewed saw their role as more than money: mentorship, network access, strategic
  partnership, and long-term flexibility.
